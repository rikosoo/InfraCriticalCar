\begin{abstract}
Vehicle cybersecurity research and regulation concentrate on the product:
ISO/SAE~21434 and UN~R155 govern the car, its ECUs and its back end. The
factory that builds the car is governed by nothing comparable, yet it is the
asset whose failure stops output, starves a continental supply chain and, in
the most expensive cyber event recorded in the United Kingdom, cost an economy
an estimated \pounds1.9 billion. This paper treats the automotive plant, rather
than the vehicle, as critical infrastructure, and asks a question that the
vehicle-centric literature cannot answer: by which paths does an attacker
actually reach the assembly line? We contribute (i)~a curated corpus of 20
publicly reported incidents in automotive manufacturing between 2017 and 2025,
each reconstructed as an asset-level path with an explicit confidence label;
(ii)~\capm, a cross-plane attack-path model that spans corporate IT, the
identity plane, cloud and SaaS, the industrial DMZ, MES/SCADA/PLC layers and
the supplier interface, instantiated as a reference architecture of 12 IEC
62443 zones, 48 assets and 91 attack steps labelled with 43 MITRE \attck{}
Enterprise and \attck{} for ICS techniques; (iii)~a probabilistic analysis
combining $k$-most-likely-path ranking with a Monte Carlo campaign simulation
that quantifies annualised loss; and (iv)~a control catalogue mapped to IEC
62443-3-3 and NIST CSF 2.0, evaluated by marginal value, necessity and
cost-effectiveness. Three results stand out. First, the path of least
resistance to a production stoppage never touches a controller: the most likely
route is $11.6\times$ more likely than the most likely route that reaches
Purdue level~2 or below, and the corpus agrees---55\% of incidents caused an
availability impact while only 10\% are reported to have interacted with
equipment below level~3. Second, recovery capability dominates prevention in
expected-loss terms: tested offline backups and a rehearsed degraded-production
mode account for the two largest single-control reductions, while SaaS token
governance and supplier assurance are the controls a mature programme can least
afford to remove. Third, partial hardening displaces rather than removes risk:
a supply-chain-and-cloud programme cuts espionage loss by 73\% but raises
ransomware loss by 26\% by pushing a rational actor onto the availability
objective. The model, corpus, experiments and figures are released as a
dependency-free open-source artefact.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A modern vehicle assembly plant is a cyber-physical system of a peculiar kind.
It is large --- tens of thousands of controlled devices across body, paint and
final assembly --- and it is tightly coupled: just-in-time and just-in-sequence
logistics remove the inventory buffers that would otherwise absorb a
disruption. It is also, increasingly, a \emph{connected} system. Manufacturing
execution systems (MES) exchange orders with enterprise resource planning (ERP)
platforms; engineering workstations are managed from the same endpoint
management infrastructure as office laptops; machine builders maintain robot
cells from another continent; production analytics run in a public cloud
tenant; suppliers receive call-offs through electronic data interchange (EDI)
interfaces that are, functionally, a conduit into the plant's scheduling logic.

Regulation has not followed this shift. The automotive sector has invested a
decade of standardisation effort in the security of the \emph{product}:
ISO/SAE~21434 defines cybersecurity engineering for road
vehicles~\cite{isosae21434} and UN~R155 makes a certified cybersecurity
management system a condition of type approval~\cite{unr155}. Both stop at the
factory gate. Conversely, the frameworks that do address industrial
environments --- IEC 62443~\cite{iec624433302020,iec624433303} and NIST
SP~800-82r3~\cite{nist80082r3} --- are sector-agnostic and organised around
zone-and-conduit segmentation, a model whose implicit threat is an adversary
descending the Purdue hierarchy~\cite{williams1994purdue} towards the process.

The empirical record does not fit that picture. Between 2017 and 2025,
automotive production was halted repeatedly --- at Renault-Nissan, Honda,
Toyota, Bridgestone, Stellantis and Jaguar Land Rover --- and in almost every
case the reported cause was the loss of \emph{information} systems, not the
manipulation of controllers. Production stopped because the MES was encrypted,
because ERP could not release parts, because a Tier-1 supplier could no longer
transmit delivery data, or because the victim itself disconnected networks as a
containment measure. The 2022 compromise of Kojima Industries, a Toyota Tier-1
supplier, stopped 28 lines across 14 Japanese plants and cost roughly 13,000
vehicles of output in a single day~\cite{inc04toyota,inc04infosec}, without any
claim that a programmable logic controller (PLC) was touched. The 2025 attack
on Jaguar Land Rover halted global production for approximately five weeks and
was assessed by the UK Cyber Monitoring Centre as a Category~3 systemic event
costing the British economy about \pounds1.9 billion and affecting more than
5,000 organisations~\cite{inc18cmc,inc18cw}.

If that is how plants actually fail, then risk models built around descent to
level~0 are optimising the wrong variable. What the sector needs is an
attack-path model that treats the corporate IT estate, the identity plane, the
cloud tenant, the SaaS perimeter, the industrial DMZ, the operations layer and
the supplier interface as a \emph{single} graph whose terminal states are
business consequences, and that can rank the routes through that graph by
likelihood and expected loss.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1 --- An empirical corpus.} We curate 20 publicly reported
    incidents in automotive manufacturing (2017--2025), covering OEMs, Tier-1
    suppliers, engineering-services providers and shared retail SaaS. Each
    record carries a reconstructed asset-level path and an explicit confidence
    label, so that the inevitable uncertainty of public reporting is visible
    rather than laundered (\cref{sec:corpus}).
  \item \textbf{C2 --- The \capm{} model.} We define a cross-plane attack-path
    model with an explicit probability semantics, and instantiate it as a
    reference architecture for a vehicle assembly plant: 12 IEC 62443 zones
    spanning five planes, 48 assets, 91 attack steps labelled with 43 \attck{}
    Enterprise and \attck{} for ICS techniques, and five terminal consequences
    (\cref{sec:model}).
  \item \textbf{C3 --- Path analysis.} We rank routes by exact
    $k$-most-likely-path enumeration and identify likelihood-weighted choke
    points, showing that the plant's true conduits of consequence are business
    processes rather than network boundaries (\cref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{C4 --- Risk quantification.} A Monte Carlo campaign simulation
    with three actor profiles converts path structure into annualised loss
    expectancy, downtime distributions and time-to-consequence
    (\cref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{C5 --- Control evaluation against standards.} A catalogue of 17
    controls, each mapped to IEC 62443-3-3 system requirements and NIST CSF 2.0
    subcategories, is evaluated by marginal value, necessity within a full
    programme, and cost-effectiveness, with a sensitivity and rank-stability
    analysis (\cref{sec:results,sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}

All code, data, experiments and figures are released as a dependency-free
artefact~\cite{capmartifact}; every number quoted in this paper is produced by
a single reproducible pipeline.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Zones, conduits and the Purdue model}

The Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture~\cite{williams1994purdue} orders
industrial assets into levels from the physical process (level~0) to enterprise
IT (level~4/5), and is the backbone of the zone-and-conduit decomposition of
IEC 62443-3-2~\cite{iec624433302020}. IEC 62443-3-3~\cite{iec624433303} then
assigns each zone a target security level (SL-T) realised by system
requirements (SR). NIST SP~800-82r3~\cite{nist80082r3} gives compatible
guidance, and the Industrial Control System Cyber Kill
Chain~\cite{assante2015killchain} describes the two-stage structure --- an IT
intrusion stage followed by an ICS attack development stage --- observed in
Stuxnet~\cite{langner2011stuxnet}, the Maroochy water
breach~\cite{slay2007maroochy}, the Ukrainian grid
attacks~\cite{eisac2016ukraine} and the historical record collected by
INL~\cite{inl2018history}. This literature is indispensable, but it is
organised around the descent to the process. Our results suggest that for
discrete manufacturing the dominant loss mechanism is a \emph{lateral}
business-process failure that never requires stage two.

\subsection{Attack graphs and probabilistic security metrics}

Attack graphs formalise the composition of individual steps into end-to-end
compromise~\cite{phillips1998graph,sheyner2002automated}, and logic-based
generators such as MulVAL~\cite{ou2005mulval} made them tractable on real
networks. Probabilistic metrics over attack graphs~\cite{wang2008attack}
attach success likelihoods to steps and aggregate them along paths;
surveys~\cite{kordy2014dag,lallie2020review,zenitani2023attack} map the design
space of attack/defence modelling. In industrial settings, attack trees and
graphs have been applied to SCADA vulnerability
assessment~\cite{byres2004attack,ten2008vulnerability}, to grid exposure
evaluation~\cite{hahn2011cyber} and to combined safety--security
analysis~\cite{kriaa2015survey}; Cherdantseva et al.~\cite{cherdantseva2016review}
review risk-assessment methods for SCADA and note the scarcity of empirically
grounded parameterisation. Quantitative risk work in the FAIR
tradition~\cite{freund2014fair,hubbard2016measure} supplies the
loss-distribution machinery we adopt. What is missing, and what we supply, is a
graph whose vertices span \emph{all five planes} of a manufacturing enterprise
and whose terminal states are production and business consequences rather than
host compromise.

\subsection{\attck{} Enterprise and \attck{} for ICS}

\attck{}~\cite{strom2018attack} and \attck{} for ICS~\cite{alexander2020icsattack}
provide the technique vocabulary used throughout this paper. Crucially, a
cross-plane path is \emph{bilingual}: it begins in Enterprise techniques
(T1566 Phishing, T1190 Exploit Public-Facing Application, T1078 Valid Accounts)
and, if it descends, continues in ICS techniques (T0886 Remote Services, T0843
Program Download, T0831 Manipulation of Control). Labelling every edge with the
matrix it belongs to lets us measure how much of the realistic risk mass is
expressible in each vocabulary --- and we find that most of it is Enterprise.

\subsection{Automotive cybersecurity: product versus plant}

ISO/SAE~21434~\cite{isosae21434} and UN~R155~\cite{unr155} regulate the
vehicle's cybersecurity engineering and management system. Sector supplier
assurance schemes address information handling in the supply chain, and IEC
62443-2-4~\cite{iec6244324} specifies requirements for IACS service providers.
Regulators have begun to treat manufacturing itself as in scope: the EU NIS~2
directive lists the manufacture of motor vehicles among the sectors it
covers~\cite{nis2directive}, and the United States classifies automobile
manufacturing within the Critical Manufacturing
Sector~\cite{cisacriticalmanufacturing}. The scholarly literature has not kept
pace: to our knowledge no prior work models the automotive \emph{plant} as a
cross-plane attack graph, calibrates it against the sector's own incident
record, and uses it to rank controls in the language of IEC 62443 and NIST CSF.
\Cref{sec:corpus} begins by establishing that record.

\section{The Plant as Critical Infrastructure:\\ An Incident Corpus, 2017--2025}
\label{sec:corpus}

\subsection{Corpus construction}

We assembled a corpus of publicly reported cybersecurity incidents whose victim
is an automotive OEM, a component supplier, an engineering-services provider to
the sector, or a shared platform on which vehicle production or distribution
depends. An incident was included when (i)~the victim or credible reporting
identified an automotive nexus, (ii)~at least one consequence was described in
enough detail to be classified, and (iii)~two independent sources or one
first-party disclosure were available. We deliberately span the full
consequence space --- availability, confidentiality of engineering data, and
personal-data exposure --- rather than restricting the corpus to production
outages, because the model must be able to express the routes an actor
\emph{did not} take as well as the ones it did. The result is 20 incidents:
two in 2017, two in 2020, five in 2022, four in 2023, three in 2024 and four in
2025~\cite{inc01renault,inc01nbc,inc03honda,inc04toyota,inc04infosec,inc05denso,%
inc06bridgestone,inc07continental,inc08tesla,inc09toyotacloud,inc10vw,inc11cdk,%
inc14yanfeng,inc17stellantis,inc18cmc,inc18cw,inc19toyotagithub,inc20mks}. The
full record, including the source URLs for each incident and the date they were
consulted, is published with the artefact~\cite{capmartifact}.

Each record carries a reconstruction of the incident as a sequence of assets in
the reference architecture of \cref{sec:model}, together with a confidence
label: \textsf{high} when the sequence follows from the victim's own
disclosure; \textsf{medium} when several independent sources agree;
\textsf{low} when the entry vector is reported but internal propagation is
inferred; \textsf{disputed} when sources disagree on the entry vector;
\textsf{attempted} when the campaign was interrupted before impact; and
\textsf{partial} when the observed consequence lies partly outside the
plant-centric scope of the model. Labelling is the honest response to a
structural problem of this research area: victims of manufacturing incidents
rarely publish forensic detail, and a model that hides the resulting
uncertainty behind confident-looking node sequences would be worse than one
that exposes it. \Cref{tab:corpus} lists the corpus.

\subsection{Descriptive findings}

Three properties of the corpus shape the rest of the paper.

\textbf{Availability dominates.} Eleven of twenty incidents (55\%) had
production stoppage as their primary consequence; five caused theft of
engineering or corporate data and four exposed customer personal data.

\textbf{The supply chain is a first-class attack surface.} Eight of twenty
incidents (40\%) traversed the supply-chain plane --- either because the victim
\emph{was} a supplier whose failure propagated to an OEM (Kojima/Toyota,
Yanfeng/Stellantis, Sensata, MKS Instruments), or because a shared platform
outside the OEM's control was the point of failure (CDK Global, the SaaS
platform behind the 2025 Stellantis disclosure).

\textbf{Operational technology is rarely the target.} Only two of the twenty
incidents (10\%) are reported to have interacted with equipment below Purdue
level~3, and in both cases the interaction was self-propagating malware
encrypting Windows-based production hosts rather than a deliberate
manipulation of control. No incident in the corpus involved a documented
adversarial program download to a controller. \Cref{fig:corpus-planes} shows
the distribution of traversed planes.

\subsection{Three case studies}

\textbf{Kojima Industries and Toyota (2022): coupling as an amplifier.}
Ransomware at a Tier-1 supplier of interior components encrypted the systems
used to receive orders and confirm deliveries. Toyota, running just-in-time
with minimal buffers, suspended 28 lines at 14 plants for a day, losing
approximately 13,000 vehicles of output~\cite{inc04toyota,inc04infosec}. The
OEM's own IT and OT estates were, by all public accounts, untouched. The
transmission mechanism was not a network conduit but a \emph{business}
conduit: the flow of call-off and delivery data on which production sequencing
depends. In model terms the entire path has three steps and never leaves
level~4/5.

\textbf{Jaguar Land Rover (2025): systemic loss from an IT-side event.}
Production stopped across the company's plants from 31 August 2025, with a
phased restart from early October. JLR has not disclosed the initial access
vector; public reporting has variously pointed to the exploitation of
Internet-facing enterprise software and to third-party credentials that were
never revoked, and we therefore label the reconstruction \textsf{disputed} and
encode the reported hypothesis while retaining the alternative as a distinct
path in the model. What is not disputed is the consequence: the Cyber
Monitoring Centre classified the event as a Category~3 systemic event with an
estimated economic cost of \pounds1.9 billion, affecting more than 5,000
organisations~\cite{inc18cmc,inc18cw}. Two features matter for modelling.
First, the outage duration --- roughly five weeks --- is an order of magnitude
longer than the 2017 worm-driven outages, which is what converts a plant
incident into a national economic event. Second, the loss was borne well
beyond the victim: a supplier ecosystem with no ability to influence JLR's
controls absorbed a large share of it.

\textbf{Cariad, Toyota Connected and the SaaS perimeter (2022--2025): the
cloud plane.} Misconfigured cloud storage exposed data from approximately
800,000 electric vehicles at Volkswagen's software subsidiary~\cite{inc10vw},
a cloud misconfiguration exposed 2.15 million Toyota customers' vehicle
location records over a decade~\cite{inc09toyotacloud}, an access key published
in a public code repository exposed a further
296,000~\cite{inc19toyotagithub}, and in 2025 a third-party SaaS platform was
the route to Stellantis customer data~\cite{inc17stellantis}. None of these
stopped a line. They matter here because they are cheap, high-yield routes that
compete for the same adversary effort as production disruption --- which, as
\cref{sec:results} shows, is precisely why hardening them changes the
\emph{mix} of consequences a plant should expect.

\section{The \capm{} Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Formal definition}

A \capm{} instance is a directed, labelled multigraph
\begin{equation}
G = (V, E, Z, \tau, p, \delta, c),
\end{equation}
where $V$ is a set of assets partitioned by $Z$ into IEC 62443 zones, and
$E \subseteq V \times V$ a set of atomic attack steps. $V$ contains a
distinguished entry vertex $v_0$ (the external actor) and a set
$I \subset V$ of terminal \emph{impact} vertices representing business
consequences. Each edge $e$ carries:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\tau(e)$, a MITRE \attck{} technique (Enterprise or ICS);
  \item $p(e) \in (0,1]$, the probability that the step succeeds given that the
    actor holds its source asset;
  \item $\delta(e) \in [0,1)$, the probability that the step is detected
    \emph{and contained} before the actor can build on it;
  \item $c(e) > 0$, the expected actor effort in hours.
\end{itemize}

A path $\pi = \langle e_1,\dots,e_n \rangle$ from $v_0$ to an impact has
likelihood
\begin{equation}
L(\pi) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(e_i)\,\bigl(1 - \delta(e_i)\bigr),
\label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
the probability that every step succeeds and none is contained. Taking
logarithms turns \cref{eq:likelihood} into the additive edge weight
\begin{equation}
w(e) = -\log\bigl(p(e)(1-\delta(e))\bigr) \geq 0,
\end{equation}
so the most likely path is a shortest path under $w$ and the $k$ most likely
paths follow from Yen's algorithm. This is the standard log-transform of
probabilistic attack-graph analysis~\cite{wang2008attack}; it makes the ranking
exact and avoids numerical underflow on long paths.

A control portfolio $P$ acts on edges through two factors. A \emph{mitigation}
$m_P(e) \in [0,1]$ removes a fraction of the step's success likelihood and a
\emph{detection uplift} $u_P(e) \in [0,1]$ converts part of the remaining
undetected mass into containment:
\begin{align}
p_P(e) &= p(e)\,(1 - m_P(e)), \\
\delta_P(e) &= \delta(e) + (1 - \delta(e))\,u_P(e).
\end{align}
When several controls apply to the same step their effects compose
multiplicatively on the residual, $1 - \prod_j (1-m_j)$, which encodes
diminishing returns from stacking overlapping controls.

Two structural metrics follow. For a ranked set of paths $\Pi$, the
\emph{criticality} of a vertex $v$ is the share of aggregate likelihood mass
traversing it,
\begin{equation}
\kappa(v) = \frac{\sum_{\pi \in \Pi,\, v \in \pi} L(\pi)}{\sum_{\pi \in \Pi} L(\pi)},
\end{equation}
and the same quantity computed over edges gives \emph{conduit criticality},
which identifies the boundaries whose hardening removes the most aggregate
risk. $\kappa$ is the attack-graph analogue of flow betweenness, weighted by
plausibility rather than by count --- an important distinction, because, as
\cref{sec:results} shows, counting paths and weighting them by likelihood give
opposite prioritisations.

\subsection{Reference architecture}

\Cref{fig:architecture} shows the instantiated plant. Beyond the classical
Purdue stack we model two planes that Purdue drawings omit and one that they
externalise:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \textbf{identity plane} (on-premises Active Directory, a cloud
    identity provider, federation trust, privileged access management, and
    dormant third-party credentials), which is where IT compromise becomes
    plant compromise;
  \item the \textbf{cloud plane} (tenant control plane, object storage and data
    lake, CI/CD pipeline, connected-vehicle back end) plus the third-party SaaS
    the enterprise does not operate;
  \item the \textbf{supply-chain plane} (Tier-1 IT estates, the EDI/JIT
    interface, machine-builder remote access, the OT software and firmware
    update channel, and removable media).
\end{itemize}

The instance comprises 12 zones, 48 assets and 91 attack steps drawn from 43
\attck{} techniques, 70 steps labelled with Enterprise techniques and 21 with
ICS techniques; 72 of the 91 steps cross a zone boundary and are therefore
conduits in the IEC 62443 sense. Five terminal consequences are modelled:
production stoppage, silent quality degradation, loss of a safety function,
theft of engineering data, and customer personal-data breach.

\subsection{Consequence and campaign model}

Each impact $i \in I$ carries a consequence model: a triangular distribution
over outage duration, a loss rate per hour of stopped production, and a
triangular distribution of fixed response, forensic, regulatory and
notification cost. Traversing a \emph{recovery-inhibiting} asset --- the backup
infrastructure, the endpoint-management platform, the OT domain controller ---
multiplies the sampled outage, reflecting the empirical difference between a
weekend outage and a five-week one. Consequence-side controls (tested offline
backups, a rehearsed manual degraded mode) shorten it.

Path ranking answers where an actor is likely to go, but not how often a plant
loses money, because a campaign is a sequential process under a time budget in
which the defender may intervene at any step. We therefore simulate campaigns.
An actor starts at $v_0$ with a budget (720 hours of hands-on-keyboard activity
by default) and repeatedly selects, from all steps available out of the assets
it holds, the one minimising
\begin{equation}
w(e) + d\bigl(\mathrm{dst}(e)\bigr) + \varepsilon,\quad
\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
\end{equation}
where $d(v)$ is the utility-weighted resistance from $v$ to the actor's
objectives --- the minimum over impacts of the residual path resistance minus
the log-utility the actor assigns to that impact --- and $\sigma$ is a policy
noise term representing imperfect adversary planning. The step consumes its
effort, succeeds with probability $p_P(e)$ and is contained with probability
$\delta_P(e)$; containment ends the campaign. We model three actor profiles by
their utility vectors over consequences: \emph{ransomware} (production
stoppage dominant, data theft as double-extortion leverage), \emph{espionage}
(engineering data dominant) and \emph{sabotage} (safety and quality dominant).
Annualised loss expectancy is $\mathrm{ALE} = \lambda \cdot \mathbb{E}[\text{loss
per campaign}]$ for a campaign frequency $\lambda$, with $\lambda = 1.5$ per
plant-year in the baseline and swept in \cref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Controls and standards mapping}

The catalogue contains 17 controls, each characterised by its mitigation,
detection uplift, recovery effect and a relative annualised cost index, and
each mapped to the IEC 62443-3-3~\cite{iec624433303} system requirements and
NIST CSF 2.0~\cite{nistcsf2} subcategories it implements (\cref{tab:controls}). The mapping is what makes
the quantitative output actionable: a plant does not buy ``a 0.55 mitigation
factor'', it funds an SR~5.1 zone-boundary project or a PR.AA-03
phishing-resistant authentication programme. Five named scenarios are
evaluated: $S_1$ identity-first, $S_2$ IEC 62443 zones and conduits, $S_3$
supply chain and cloud, $S_4$ detect and recover, and $S_5$ the full
programme, against the baseline $S_0$.

\subsection{Parameterisation}

Edge parameters are expert-elicited point estimates, anchored in three ways.
Initial-access probabilities are ordered by the relative frequency of the
corresponding entry vectors in the corpus and in public reporting on
manufacturing intrusions. Lateral and descent probabilities reflect the
technical preconditions of the step --- whether it requires only valid
credentials, an exploit, or physical presence. Detection probabilities encode
the baseline monitoring maturity a mid-sized OEM plant can be assumed to
possess \emph{before} any catalogue control is deployed, which is why $S_0$ is
described as a baseline rather than an unprotected configuration. Consequence
parameters are anchored on reported outcomes: the one-day, 14-plant Toyota
stoppage at one end of the duration distribution and the five-week JLR outage
at the other.

We make no claim that these point estimates are the true parameters of any
specific plant. The appropriate response to elicited parameters is not to
apologise for them but to test what they support: \cref{sec:results} therefore
reports how conclusions move under systematic perturbation, and treats any
finding that does not survive that perturbation as unsupported.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experiments}

Six experiments run over the same graph instance. \textbf{E1} enumerates every
loop-free path of at most twelve steps from the entry vertex to a consequence,
ranks paths by \cref{eq:likelihood}, and measures the depth each path reaches
in the Purdue hierarchy. \textbf{E2} reconciles the corpus with the model:
whether each reconstructed incident is a path of the model, where that path
falls in the model's own ranking, and how the model behaves under an ablation
described below. \textbf{E3} computes vertex and conduit criticality.
\textbf{E4} runs the campaign simulation for three actor profiles across the
six control scenarios. \textbf{E5} evaluates each control three ways: its
\emph{marginal} value when deployed alone on the baseline, its
\emph{necessity} measured as the loss that returns when it is withdrawn from
the full programme, and its cost-effectiveness; it then builds a greedy
portfolio by repeatedly adding the control with the best risk reduction per
unit cost. \textbf{E6} tests robustness: the stability of the path ranking
under multiplicative log-normal perturbation of every $p$ and $\delta$
($\sigma = 0.35$, 200 replicates), one-at-a-time sweeps of detection efficacy,
campaign frequency and actor budget, and the stability of the control ranking
across twelve perturbed graphs.

Monte Carlo experiments use 20,000 campaigns per configuration with fixed
seeds; the full pipeline runs in about nine minutes on a single core of a
commodity machine and requires only a CPython~3.9+ interpreter. Because the
corpus informed the construction of the graph, plain coverage would be a
consistency check rather than a validation. E2 therefore adds a leave-one-out
ablation: for each incident, every edge whose \emph{only} supporting evidence
is that incident is deleted and the incident is re-tested against the reduced
model. A path that survives is expressed by structure that generalises across
cases rather than by steps fitted to a single one.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{F1: the path of least resistance bypasses the control layers}

The reference architecture admits 681 distinct attack paths of at most twelve
steps: 455 reach production stoppage, 118 quality degradation, 52 engineering
data theft, 35 a safety consequence and 21 a personal-data breach. Counted
naively, the plant looks like a classical OT problem: 379 of the 455 production
paths (83\%) pass through Purdue level~2 or below.

Weighting by likelihood inverts that conclusion. \Cref{fig:top-paths} and
\cref{tab:top-paths} give the ten most likely routes to production stoppage.
The most likely path has three steps and a likelihood of $0.047$:
\emph{external actor $\to$ Tier-1 supplier IT $\to$ EDI/JIT interface $\to$
production stoppage}. The second, at $0.041$, is \emph{external actor $\to$
Internet-facing enterprise application $\to$ ERP $\to$ production stoppage}.
The most likely path that actually reaches level~2 or below has a likelihood of
$0.0040$ --- the highest-likelihood route to a stopped line is $11.6\times$
more likely than the highest-likelihood route that touches supervisory control
or below.

\subsection{F2: the model agrees with the record}

All 20 corpus incidents are expressible as paths of the reference model. More
informative is where those paths fall in the model's own ranking: the median
reconstructed incident sits at the $4.8$th percentile of all modelled paths
reaching the same consequence, and all 20 fall within the top quartile. The
routes that actually occurred are the routes the model considers most likely,
which is the behaviour a usable prioritisation instrument must exhibit.

Under the leave-one-out ablation, 13 of 20 incidents (65\%) remain expressible
after deleting every step that exists solely on their own evidence, rising to
75\% when only internal propagation steps are ablated and initial access is
retained. The failures are informative: they are almost entirely
\emph{initial-access} steps --- the drive-by download behind the Continental
intrusion, the insider recruitment attempted at Tesla, the dealer-platform
compromise at CDK, the SaaS token abuse behind the Stellantis disclosure, the
exposed cloud secret in a public repository. Entry vectors in this sector are
idiosyncratic and each new campaign tends to introduce one; what generalises is
what happens afterwards. That asymmetry has a direct defensive reading, taken
up in \cref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{F3: the choke points are business systems}

\Cref{fig:chokepoints} ranks assets by the share of aggregate path likelihood
traversing them. Excluding the consequence vertices themselves, the leading
choke points are the engineering workstation (0.27), the employee workstation
(0.23), the remote-access VPN gateway (0.16), the Tier-1 supplier IT estate
(0.15) and the ERP core (0.14). At the conduit level the highest-criticality
transitions are \emph{actor $\to$ Tier-1 supplier} via T1566 (0.15),
\emph{actor $\to$ Internet-facing application} via T1190 (0.11) and
\emph{actor $\to$ VPN} via T1133 (0.11).

The engineering workstation deserves emphasis. It is the one asset that appears
at high criticality in \emph{both} regimes: it is the hinge of the descent into
control (every program-download path passes through it) and it is reachable
from the corporate estate through endpoint management, flat-network
exploitation, vendor remote access, removable media and insider access. In IEC
62443 terms it is a level~3 asset routinely administered from level~4 --- a
zone whose SL-T is undermined by the conduits its administrators require.

\subsection{F4: scenario risk and the displacement effect}

\Cref{fig:scenario-ale} and \cref{tab:scenarios} report campaign outcomes. In
the baseline against the ransomware profile, 9.8\% of campaigns reach a
consequence, 7.7\% cause a production stoppage, and 90.2\% are contained before
any consequence. Mean loss is \$31.7M per campaign and \$323M conditional on
impact, with a 95th percentile of \$733M; mean time from first action to first
consequence is 35 hours. At $\lambda = 1.5$ campaigns per plant-year this is an
annualised loss expectancy of \$47.6M.

Two scenario results are worth stating precisely.

\textbf{Detection and recovery dominate.} $S_4$ --- endpoint detection with
monitored response, immutable and exercised backups, attack-surface management
and operational resilience --- reduces ALE by 64.6\% to \$16.9M. The full
programme $S_5$ reduces it by 92.9\% to \$3.4M. By contrast, $S_1$
(identity-first) and $S_2$ (IEC 62443 zones and conduits) each move baseline ALE
by about 1\%. This is not an argument against identity
hardening or segmentation, which are the controls that make the \emph{OT}
paths expensive; it is a statement about where the expected loss currently
sits.

\textbf{Partial hardening displaces risk.} $S_3$ (supply chain and cloud) cuts
espionage-profile ALE from \$22.0M to \$5.9M --- a 73\% reduction --- while
\emph{raising} ransomware-profile ALE by 26.3\%, from \$47.6M to \$60.1M. The
mechanism is visible in the simulation traces: in the baseline, a utility-aware
actor frequently detours to cheap data-theft objectives and is often contained
during those detours; removing the cheap objectives concentrates the campaign
on the most damaging one. Risk displacement of this kind is invisible to
control assessments that score each objective independently, and it argues for
sequencing a programme so that availability protection does not lag
confidentiality protection.

\subsection{F5: which controls matter, and in what sense}

Deployed alone on the baseline, only four controls produce a material ALE
reduction against the ransomware profile: operational resilience $C_{17}$
(\$20.2M), immutable and exercised backups $C_{08}$ (\$18.3M), attack-surface
management $C_{10}$ (\$5.5M) and monitored endpoint detection $C_{07}$
(\$4.9M). Every other control, taken alone, is worth close to nothing --- not
because it is ineffective on the steps it covers, but because the graph offers
redundant routes around it. The best single-control reductions of the
\emph{leading path likelihood} are supplier assurance $C_{14}$ ($-12.8\%$) and
operational resilience $C_{17}$ ($-10.0\%$); a control can bend the top route
substantially and still barely move expected loss.

Necessity tells the complementary story (\cref{fig:necessity}). Withdrawing a
single control from the full programme $S_5$ costs most for SaaS and OAuth
token governance $C_{11}$ ($+\$12.9$M) and supplier security assurance
$C_{14}$ ($+\$11.8$M), followed by monitored detection $C_{07}$ ($+\$1.6$M)
and cloud posture management $C_{16}$ ($+\$1.3$M). The controls that are
worthless alone are the ones a mature programme cannot remove: they are the
walls that close the routes the other controls push the actor towards. Marginal
analysis alone --- the standard way security investments are justified ---
systematically undervalues them.

\input{figures/fig_necessity.tex}

Greedy selection by risk reduction per unit cost yields, for the ransomware
profile, the sequence $C_{08}$ (backups), $C_{17}$ (operational resilience),
$C_{11}$ (SaaS token governance), $C_{10}$ (attack-surface management),
$C_{14}$ (supplier assurance), $C_{02}$ (conditional access), taking ALE from
\$47.6M to \$5.5M for 31 of the 91 units of cost index that the full catalogue
would consume --- an 88\% reduction, which is 95\% of the reduction achieved by
the complete 17-control programme for roughly a third of its cost
(\cref{fig:frontier}, \cref{tab:greedy}). The ordering is profile-dependent:
against the sabotage profile the OT-side controls that barely register for
ransomware enter the frontier much earlier, which is the quantitative form of
the observation that a plant's control priorities depend on who is expected to
attack it.

\subsection{F6: what survives perturbation}

Under multiplicative log-normal perturbation of every probability parameter
($\sigma = 0.35$, 200 replicates), the top-ten ranking of production-stoppage
paths retains on average 65.7\% of its members, and the single most likely path
remains first in 36\% of replicates. The \emph{qualitative} finding is far more
stable than the ordering: in every replicate the leading paths remain
short, IT- and supply-chain-side routes, because the deep OT routes are longer
and multiplicative penalties compound with length. Conclusions about which
\emph{class} of route dominates are supported; claims about the precise
ordering within the top ten are not, and we do not make them.

One-at-a-time sweeps (\cref{fig:sensitivity}) identify detection efficacy as
the dominant parameter: halving every $\delta$ raises baseline ALE from \$47.6M
to \$176.8M, a factor of $3.7$, while increasing it by 50\% lowers ALE to
\$14.2M. ALE is linear in campaign frequency by construction, so the range
\$15.9M--\$126.8M across $\lambda \in [0.5, 4.0]$ measures the cost of
uncertainty about targeting rather than about the plant. Across twelve
perturbed graphs, the control ranking by cost-effectiveness is led consistently
by $C_{08}$ (mean rank 2.3), $C_{17}$ (3.3) and $C_{10}$ (3.8); supplier
assurance $C_{14}$ and SaaS governance $C_{11}$ have high variance (best rank
1, worst rank 17), reflecting genuine sensitivity to how exposed a given plant's
supplier and SaaS interfaces are.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The conduits that matter are business processes}

IEC 62443 asks an organisation to define zones and the conduits between them,
and practitioners instinctively read ``conduit'' as ``network path''. The
results argue for a broader reading. The single most likely route to a stopped
line in our model, and the route taken in the most consequential supply-chain
incident in the corpus, is a \emph{data-flow} conduit: the stream of call-offs,
sequencing instructions and delivery confirmations between a supplier and the
plant. It carries no control traffic, crosses no OT firewall and is invisible
to an asset-centric zone model, yet its interruption stops production as
effectively as a controller failure. The same is true of the ERP--MES coupling.
A 62443 risk assessment that enumerates only technical conduits will assign
these flows no SL-T and no monitoring requirement.

The practical recommendation is to extend the zone-and-conduit exercise with a
\emph{production-dependency} conduit inventory: for each business data flow on
which sequencing, parts availability or dispatch depends, record its source
zone, its owner (often a third party), the maximum tolerable interruption, and
the manual fallback. In our model this is exactly what control $C_{17}$
represents, and it is the second-best single investment available.

\subsection{Recovery is a security control}

The two largest single-control reductions in expected loss are tested offline
backups and a rehearsed degraded-production mode --- both of which act on
consequence, not on likelihood. This is not an artefact of our parameters but a
structural property of a tightly coupled system with a heavy-tailed outage
distribution: when mean loss is dominated by the tail, shortening the tail
beats shaving the probability of entering it. The empirical record points the
same way. The 2017 worm outages lasted a weekend; the 2025 JLR outage lasted
five weeks and became a national economic event~\cite{inc18cmc}. The difference
between those two outcomes is a recovery difference, not a prevention
difference.

A corollary for governance: recovery capability should be audited with the same
rigour as preventive controls, which in practice means evidence of restoration
exercises that include OT artefacts --- controller projects, HMI images, MES
databases, recipe and parameter sets --- and evidence that a plant can run in a
degraded mode without its MES for a defined period.

\subsection{Prioritisation is adversary-dependent, and partial programmes
displace risk}

The greedy frontier differs across actor profiles, and $S_3$ lowers espionage
loss while raising ransomware loss. Both findings caution against a single
ranked control list presented as universally optimal. A plant that models only
one adversary will systematically misprice the controls that matter to another,
and a programme that hardens confidentiality-oriented routes first can increase
availability risk in the interim. The defensible position is to sequence
investments so that consequence-side controls --- which are objective-agnostic
because they act after the actor has succeeded --- are in place before
selectively hardening one objective's routes.

\subsection{Entry is idiosyncratic; propagation is not}

The leave-one-out ablation showed that incidents fail to be reproducible mostly
at their first step. Each campaign in this sector seems to arrive through its
own door: a drive-by download, a vishing call to a service desk, an exposed
enterprise application, a dormant contractor account, an OAuth token in a SaaS
integration, a secret in a public repository, an insider offered a million
dollars. Internal propagation, by contrast, reuses a small repertoire. A
security programme that spends its marginal effort trying to enumerate entry
vectors is fighting a long tail; the same effort spent on the propagation
repertoire --- credential tiering, endpoint management hygiene, the engineering
workstation, the ERP--MES coupling, recovery --- defends against doors that
have not been invented yet. This is an old argument, but the corpus and the
ablation give it a concrete, sector-specific measurement.

\subsection{Sector-level implications}

Two corpus incidents --- Kojima/Toyota and CDK Global --- share a structure no
single-enterprise risk model can capture: the failure of one organisation
propagated to hundreds or thousands of others through a shared dependency. JLR
extends the pattern to a national scale, with more than 5,000 organisations
affected~\cite{inc18cmc,inc18cw}. Regulatory frameworks are beginning to
recognise vehicle manufacturing as in scope for critical-infrastructure
obligations~\cite{nis2directive,cisacriticalmanufacturing}, but obligations
placed on individual entities do not by themselves address concentration risk
in shared platforms. The natural extension of \capm{} is a sector-level graph
in which OEMs, Tier-$n$ suppliers and shared platforms are vertices and
just-in-time coupling is an explicit amplification parameter; the model as
specified already treats the supplier interface as a first-class plane, so the
extension is mechanical rather than conceptual.

\subsection{Applying the model in a plant}

The intended workflow for a practitioner is: (1) replace the reference asset
set with the plant's own inventory, preserving the five planes; (2) mark each
edge as present, absent or conditionally present --- absence of an edge is a
security claim, and writing it down converts an assumption into an auditable
statement; (3) re-elicit $p$, $\delta$ and effort with plant engineers, using
purple-team results where available; (4) run the pipeline; (5) read the outputs
as a ranked conduit and control agenda expressed in IEC 62443 and NIST CSF
terms. The value of the exercise is less in the absolute loss figures --- which
inherit all the uncertainty of their inputs --- than in the relative ordering
and, above all, in the disagreements it surfaces between what a plant believes
about its segmentation and what its own conduit inventory implies.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:validity}

\textbf{Construct validity.} Edge probabilities are expert-elicited point
estimates rather than measured frequencies; no public dataset supports
per-technique success rates in automotive OT environments. We mitigate this by
publishing every parameter, by reporting perturbation results, and by
restricting our claims to those that survive perturbation. The consequence
model assumes losses accrue linearly in outage duration, which understates the
non-linear effects of missed launch dates and contractual penalties.

\textbf{Internal validity.} The campaign policy is a noisy best-first search
under a utility vector. Real actors are less consistent and occasionally more
creative; in particular, the displacement effect of \cref{sec:results} depends
on the assumption that an actor rationally re-targets when cheap objectives are
removed. Steps are treated as conditionally independent given the actor's
holdings, which ignores correlated failures such as a single credential
unlocking several conduits at once; this biases likelihoods downward for
identity-centric routes.

\textbf{External validity.} The corpus rests on public reporting, which is
biased towards incidents large enough to disturb production or trigger
disclosure, and towards Europe, North America and Japan. Silent intrusions,
successful defences and incidents at smaller Tier-$n$ suppliers are
systematically under-represented, so the model is calibrated on the visible
part of the distribution. The reference architecture describes a
high-automation vehicle assembly plant; a stamping plant, a battery cell
factory or a low-automation facility would require a different instance,
although we expect the five-plane structure to transfer.

\textbf{Conclusion validity.} Monte Carlo estimates use 20,000 campaigns per
configuration with fixed seeds; differences of less than roughly 3\% in ALE
between neighbouring configurations should not be interpreted. The corpus, at
20 incidents, supports descriptive proportions with wide confidence intervals;
we therefore report counts alongside percentages throughout.

\textbf{Ethics.} The corpus is built exclusively from public reporting, and the
model deliberately operates at the level of techniques and conduits rather than
exploits: no vulnerability details, payloads or procedures that would assist an
attacker appear in the paper or in the released artefact. The reference
architecture is generic and does not describe any identifiable plant.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We set out to model the automotive plant, rather than the vehicle, as critical
infrastructure, and to answer a question the vehicle-centric literature does
not address: which paths actually reach the assembly line. The answer, on both
the empirical record and the model, is that the shortest route to a stopped
line runs through business and logistics systems --- a supplier's EDI
interface, an Internet-facing enterprise application, an ERP core, an MES ---
and not through a controller. The most likely route to a production stoppage is
$11.6\times$ more likely than the most likely route that reaches supervisory
control or below; 55\% of the corpus incidents caused an availability impact
while only 10\% are reported to have interacted with equipment below level~3.
Detection efficacy is the parameter that matters most, recovery capability is
the single most valuable investment class, controls that look worthless in
isolation turn out to be the least removable ones in a mature programme, and
partial hardening can displace risk from one objective onto a more damaging
one.

Four extensions follow directly. First, \emph{calibration}: replacing elicited
probabilities with measurements from purple-team exercises and plant telemetry,
and updating them in a Bayesian fashion as evidence accumulates. Second,
\emph{sector-level modelling}: lifting the graph from one plant to a network of
OEMs, suppliers and shared platforms with explicit just-in-time coupling, which
is the only level at which the Kojima, CDK and JLR class of event can be priced.
Third, \emph{live instantiation}: deriving edges from asset-inventory and
network-flow data rather than from expert elicitation, so that the model tracks
a plant as it changes. Fourth, \emph{validation in a testbed}: executing the
top-ranked paths against a physical or digital-twin line to measure the
parameters this paper had to elicit. The artefact accompanying this
paper~\cite{capmartifact} is structured so that each of these is an extension
of the existing pipeline rather than a rewrite.
